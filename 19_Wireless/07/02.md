To achieve smooth 802.11 roaming in a home environment (preventing dropped video calls or lag when walking between rooms),
you need to configure your home network so that multiple Access Points (APs) cooperate seamlessly.

Implementing proper roaming at home involves choosing the right hardware setup and configuring the network parameters
correctly.

---

### 1. Choose Your Hardware Architecture

To enable multiple coverage points under a single network name (SSID), you need one of two setups:

- **Wi-Fi Mesh System (Recommended for Most Homes):** Systems like Eero, Google Nest, TP-Link Deco, or ASUS AiMesh consist of
  multiple nodes that talk to each other wirelessly or via Ethernet. They natively handle seamless handoffs and protocol
  standards like 802.11k/r/v automatically.
- **Multiple Wired Access Points:** If you have Ethernet cables wired through your house, you can connect multiple
  independent Access Points (such as Ubiquiti UniFi, TP-Link Omada, or business-grade APs) to a central switch or router, all
  broadcasting the exact same SSID and security key.

---

### 2. Enable Fast Roaming Standards (802.11r / k / v)

If you are using a prosumer or enterprise-grade system (like UniFi or Omada), you must explicitly enable the roaming
protocols in your controller settings:

- **802.11k (Neighbor Reports):** Tells your phone or laptop which nearby AP channels to scan, saving battery and scanning
  time.
- **802.11r (Fast BSS Transition / FT):** Pre-authenticates encryption keys across APs so the handoff happens in
  milliseconds. _(Note: Ensure your older smart home or IoT devices support 802.11r before enabling it globally, as some
  legacy devices fail to connect)._
- **802.11v (BSS Transition Management):** Allows the network to politely nudge or steer "sticky" clients away from a
  distant, weak AP toward a closer, stronger one.

---

### 3. Optimize Access Point Placement and Power

Even with the best protocols enabled, poor physical placement will ruin roaming performance because **the client device
ultimately decides when to switch APs**.

- **Avoid Overlapping Too Much Power:** If your router's transmission (Tx) power is set too high, your phone will stubbornly
  hold onto a distant AP because it can still _hear_ it, even though the connection speed has tanked. Lower the 2.4 GHz and 5
  GHz radio power levels slightly so devices are forced to look for a closer AP.
- **Maintain Line of Sight / Proper Spacing:** Place satellite nodes or APs roughly 1 to 2 rooms apart. Avoid hiding nodes
  inside metal cabinets or thick concrete structural walls.

---

### 4. Use a Single SSID and Band Steering

- **Unified SSID:** Ensure all your APs broadcast the exact same Wi-Fi name (SSID) and use the exact same security passphrase
  (e.g., WPA2/WPA3).
- **Band Steering:** Enable band steering on your router settings if available. This pushes capable devices automatically
  from the congested 2.4 GHz band (which travels far but is slow) onto the faster 5 GHz or 6 GHz bands when you are close to
  an AP.
