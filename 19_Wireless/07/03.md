In wireless networking, **RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator)** and **client roaming thresholds** work together to keep
wireless devices connected to the best possible Access Point (AP) as they move around.

---

### 1. What is RSSI?

RSSI is a measurement of how well a client device (like a phone or laptop) can hear the radio signal from an Access Point, or
vice versa.

- **Measurement Unit:** Expressed in **dBm** (decibels-milliwatts). Because wireless power is a fraction of a milliwatt,
  these numbers are **negative**.
- **Closer to 0 = Stronger Signal:** A value of `-30 dBm` is extremely strong (standing right next to the router), while
  `-90 dBm` is barely perceptible and usually results in a dropped connection.

#### Typical RSSI Benchmarks:

- **-30 to -50 dBm:** Excellent signal
- **-60 to -67 dBm:** Very good signal (ideal for heavy tasks like voice and video calls)
- **-70 dBm:** Acceptable for basic web browsing
- **-80 dBm and lower:** Weak, borderline, or unusable reliability

---

### 2. Client Roaming and Thresholds

**Roaming** is the process where a client device drops its connection to one Access Point and attaches to another without
interrupting user activity.

By default, roaming is **client-driven**. The wireless card inside your phone or laptop constantly listens to surrounding
APs. When the signal from the current AP drops below a certain trigger point (its internal roaming threshold, typically
around `-70 dBm` to `-75 dBm`), the client looks for a better AP and initiates a roam.

#### The "Sticky Client" Problem

Many client devices are stubborn (often called "sticky clients"). They will hold onto a weak connection from a distant AP
simply because they can still hear it, even if a closer, much stronger AP is right next to them. This results in slow speeds,
high packet loss, and lagging applications.

---

### 3. Managing Roaming with Thresholds

To prevent sticky clients and network degradation, network administrators use infrastructure-side settings:

- **RSSI Thresholds / Min RSSI:** Configured on the APs themselves. If an AP notices that a client's signal has dropped below
  a specific threshold (e.g., `-75 dBm`), the AP will actively disassociate (kick) the client. This forces the device to wake
  up, scan the area, and latch onto a healthier, nearby AP.
- **Cell Overlap Design:** Wireless networks are designed with about 15% to 20% cell overlap between neighboring APs. This
  ensures that as a client leaves one AP's strong coverage zone, it immediately enters another strong zone before the signal
  degrades too much.
