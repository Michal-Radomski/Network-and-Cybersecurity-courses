Access points (APs) can be categorized based on their deployment architecture, management style, and physical capabilities.
Choosing the right type depends on the size of the network, budget, and management requirements.

---

## 1. Classification by Architecture and Management

### Autonomous (Standalone) Access Points

- **What they are:** Traditional APs that act as independent, standalone devices.
- **How they work:** Each AP must be configured and managed individually through a web interface, command-line interface
  (CLI), or SSH.
- **Best used for:** Small offices, home networks, or simple deployments where managing a few devices manually is practical.

### Controller-Managed (Unified) Access Points

- **What they are:** APs that rely on a physical or virtual central wireless LAN controller (WLC).
- **How they work:** The APs function as "lightweight" devices (sometimes called LAPW). All configuration, security policies,
  roaming management, and firmware updates are handled centrally by the controller and pushed down to the APs.
- **Best used for:** Enterprise environments, campuses, and large corporate offices with dozens or hundreds of APs.

### Cloud-Managed Access Points

- **What they are:** Modern APs managed entirely through a cloud-based dashboard (such as Cisco Meraki, Aruba Central, or
  Ubiquiti UniFi).
- **How they work:** The APs connect over the internet to a cloud platform, allowing administrators to monitor, configure,
  and troubleshoot networks remotely from anywhere in the world.
- **Best used for:** Multi-site businesses, retail chains, and growing organizations that want enterprise-grade features
  without maintaining physical on-premise hardware controllers.

---

## 2. Classification by Deployment and Use Case

### Indoor Access Points

- **What they are:** Standard APs designed for office ceilings, walls, or desktop placements.
- **Features:** Built with aesthetic enclosures and internal omnidirectional antennas to broadcast Wi-Fi evenly across a room
  or hallway.

### Outdoor Access Points

- **What they are:** Ruggedized APs built to withstand extreme weather conditions, including rain, snow, extreme
  temperatures, and lightning strikes.
- **Features:** Weatherproof housings (often IP67 rated) and ports for external directional or sector antennas to cover large
  open areas like parking lots, stadiums, or between buildings.

### Wall Plate (Hospitality) Access Points

- **What they are:** Compact APs designed to replace standard Ethernet wall jacks.
- **Features:** They provide Wi-Fi coverage for a single hotel room or dorm while also offering physical Ethernet ports
  underneath for wired devices like smart TVs or VoIP phones.

---

## 3. Classification by Functionality

### Root Access Points (Bridge/WDS Master)

- **What they are:** APs directly connected to the wired network infrastructure (such as the main router or switch).
- **Role:** They act as the primary gateway providing internet and network access to wireless clients or linking to other
  wireless bridges.

### Repeater / Extender Access Points

- **What they are:** APs configured to extend the range of an existing wireless network without a wired connection.
- **Role:** They capture the wireless signal from a root AP and retransmit it to reach dead zones (though this typically
  halves the available bandwidth per hop).

### Mesh Access Points

- **What they are:** APs that dynamically form a self-healing, multi-node wireless web (mesh) to pass data seamlessly across
  a coverage area.
- **Role:** Unlike simple repeaters, mesh nodes communicate efficiently with one another to automatically route traffic along
  the fastest path back to the wired gateway.
