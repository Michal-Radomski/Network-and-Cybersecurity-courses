A **passphrase** is generally more secure than a traditional password primarily because of its **length and structure**,
which makes it exponentially harder for automated systems to crack through brute-force or dictionary attacks, while still
being much easier for a human to remember.

---

### Key Comparison: Passwords vs. Passphrases

| Feature                  | Traditional Password                                                                       | Passphrase                                                                      |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Typical Length**       | 8 – 12 characters                                                                          | 20 – 50+ characters (multiple words)                                            |
| **Composition**          | Mixed case, numbers, symbols (e.g., `P@ssw0rd!`)                                           | A sequence of random words (e.g., `correct horse battery staple`)               |
| **Human Memory**         | Harder to remember due to arbitrary complexity; often leads to reuse or writing them down. | Easier to visualize and remember as a phrase or sentence.                       |
| **Entropy (Randomness)** | Lower, because hackers know humans use predictable substitutions (like `@` for `a`).       | Higher, due to the sheer number of possible combinations across multiple words. |

---

### Why Passphrases Win on Security

#### 1. Exponentially Greater Length

- **The Math:** Computer security relies on time and computing power to guess combinations (brute-forcing). Every additional
  character drastically increases the number of possible combinations.
- **Example:** A short password like `Tr0ub4dor&3` (10 characters) can often be cracked by modern GPUs in a matter of hours
  or days. A four- or five-word passphrase like `correct horse battery staple` creates a string of nearly 30 characters,
  pushing the time required to crack it into millions of years.

#### 2. Resistance to Automated Cracking

- **Pattern Recognition:** Hackers use specialized tools loaded with dictionaries and common substitution rules (e.g.,
  knowing that people substitute `3` for `E`, `!` for `I`, or `0` for `O`). These tools crack traditional complex passwords
  surprisingly fast.
- **Random Words:** Passphrases rely on completely random, unrelated words strung together. Because there is no logical
  grammar or predictable substitution pattern, cracking algorithms cannot use standard shortcut dictionaries effectively.

#### 3. Better Human Habits

- **The Problem with Complexity:** When forced to use short, highly complex passwords, users tend to create weak variations
  of the same password across multiple sites, or write them down on sticky notes.
- **The Passphrase Advantage:** Because passphrases are naturally easier to recall, users are far less likely to write them
  down or reuse them across different accounts.

---

### Best Practices for Creating a Secure Passphrase

- **Use 4 or More Words:** Aim for a minimum length of 20 characters by combining at least four random words.
- **Ensure True Randomness:** Avoid predictable phrases, famous quotes, or lyrics. The words should ideally have no logical
  connection to one another.
- **Avoid Personal Information:** Do not include easily guessable details such as your pet's name, birth year, or favorite
  sports team.
- **Use a Password Manager:** To keep track of your passphrases securely without having to memorize dozens of them, store
  them in a reputable password manager.
