### What is MAC Spoofing?

**MAC Spoofing** (Media Access Control spoofing) is a technique where an attacker changes or "clones" the unique MAC address
assigned to their network interface card (NIC) to mimic the MAC address of another, legitimate device on a Wireless Local
Area Network (WLAN).

Every hardware device with a network adapter has a globally unique 48-bit MAC address burned into its hardware by the
manufacturer. While these addresses are technically meant to be permanent, operating systems allow users to easily
temporarily change or "spoof" them via software commands.

---

### How MAC Spoofing Works in WLANs

Unlike wired networks—where traffic is physically segregated by switches—wireless networks broadcast frames over the air,
making them inherently open to interception and impersonation. The mechanics of MAC spoofing typically follow these steps:

1. **Information Gathering (Sniffing):** An attacker listens passively to wireless traffic using a packet analyzer (like
   Wireshark or Aircrack-ng) to observe the MAC addresses of active clients and the access point (AP).
2. **Identifying a Target:** The attacker notes a trusted device that has successfully authenticated and connected to the
   WLAN (for example, an office laptop, a smartphone, or a printer).
3. **Changing the MAC Address:** The attacker uses command-line tools (such as `macchanger` on Linux or network adapter
   properties in Windows) to change their own wireless card's MAC address to match the target's MAC address.
4. **Impersonation and Disassociation:** To take over the connection, the attacker often sends a **Deauthentication (Deauth)
   attack** frame to forcefully kick the legitimate device off the network. Once the real device disconnects, the attacker's
   spoofed device steps into its place, tricking the router into treating the attacker as the authorized user.

---

### Why Attackers Use MAC Spoofing in WLANs

Attackers and penetration testers use MAC spoofing in wireless environments for several reasons:

- **Bypassing MAC Address Filtering:** Many home and enterprise routers use "MAC white-listing" (allowing only pre-approved
  MAC addresses to join the network). Spoofing a trusted MAC address allows an unauthorized device to bypass this filter.
- **Evading Detection and Captive Portals:** Public Wi-Fi networks (like those in airports or cafes) often track users or
  limit internet access based on MAC addresses. Spoofing allows an attacker to bypass time limits, payment walls, or
  monitoring rules.
- **Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Attacks:** By mimicking a legitimate client or even an access point, an attacker can intercept,
  view, or alter data flowing between the victim and the network.

---

### Limitations and Countermeasures

While MAC spoofing is a useful tactic for malicious actors, it has distinct limitations:

- **No Encryption Bypass:** MAC spoofing only masks _identity_ at Layer 2 (Data Link Layer); it does **not** grant access to
  encrypted networks on its own. If a network uses strong encryption like WPA2 or WPA3, the attacker still needs the correct
  Wi-Fi password or handshake to decrypt traffic.
- **Duplicate Address Conflicts:** If both the real device and the spoofed device try to communicate on the network
  simultaneously, it creates packet collisions and erratic behavior that alert network administrators.

#### How to Defend Against It:

- **Use Strong Encryption:** Modern protocols like WPA2 and WPA3 integrate cryptographic authentication and dynamic key
  management, making simple MAC spoofing insufficient for network access.
- **Management Frame Protection (PMF):** Enabled in WPA2/WPA3, PMF protects management frames (like deauthentication frames)
  using cryptography, preventing attackers from easily kicking legitimate users offline.
- **Enterprise Authentication (WPA3-Enterprise / 802.1X):** Moving away from a shared static password to individual user
  credentials (certificates or login credentials) ensures that access is tied to user identity rather than hardware
  addresses.
