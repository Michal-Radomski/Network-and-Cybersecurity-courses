### What is WLAN Bridging?

**WLAN (Wireless Local Area Network) bridging** is a method used to connect two or more separate wired networks or network
segments together using a wireless radio link instead of a physical Ethernet cable.

While standard access points connect _wireless clients_ to a _wired network_, a wireless bridge connects _two separate wired
networks_ (or remote buildings) across open space through the air.

---

### How WLAN Bridging Works

1. **Point-to-Point or Point-to-Multipoint:** Specialized high-gain directional antennas are placed on buildings or towers
   within line-of-sight of one another.
2. **Layer 2 Transparency:** The bridge operates at the Data Link layer (Layer 2) of the OSI model. It transparently forwards
   Ethernet frames between the two wired switches, making the remote building act as if it is physically plugged into the
   main switch via a long invisible cable.
3. **Dedicated Backhaul:** These links typically use dedicated radio frequencies (such as 5 GHz, 60 GHz, or outdoor microwave
   bands) separated from regular client Wi-Fi to maximize throughput and stability.

---

### Common Types of WLAN Bridges

- **Point-to-Point (PtP) Bridge:** Connects two specific locations directly (e.g., bridging a network connection from a main
  house to a detached garage or from one corporate office building to another across a parking lot).
- **Point-to-Multipoint (PtMP) Bridge:** Connects a central "hub" location to multiple remote locations simultaneously (e.g.,
  a school district connecting multiple portable classrooms back to a main administration server).

---

### Key Benefits

- **Cost-Effective for Difficult Terrain:** Avoids the high labor and material costs of trenching cables underground, running
  lines across public property, or paying for leased fiber optic lines.
- **High Bandwidth:** Modern outdoor bridge hardware can deliver gigabit-plus speeds over long distances (ranging from
  hundreds of meters to several miles) with very low latency.
- **Easy Extension:** Allows full network expansion—including printers, IP security cameras, and local switches—to remote
  areas that are difficult to wire conventionally.
