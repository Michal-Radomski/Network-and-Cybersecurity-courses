A sample represents the **amplitude (instantaneous level) of the audio signal at one particular moment in time**.

The important part is that a sample is **not a tiny piece of sound**. It is a number describing the waveform at a particular
point.

### Imagine a microphone

A sound wave makes air pressure change:

```text
air pressure
    ↑
    |       /\        /\
    |      /  \      /  \
  0 |-----/----\----/----\------→ time
    |    /      \  /
    |   /        \/
    ↓
```

A microphone converts those pressure changes into an electrical voltage:

```text
voltage
    ↑
    |       /\        /\
    |      /  \      /  \
  0 |-----/----\----/----\------→ time
    |    /      \  /
    |   /        \/
```

An ADC (analog-to-digital converter) measures that voltage thousands of times per second.

For example, at **44.1 kHz**:

```text
time:       0    1    2    3    4    5    6
            ↓    ↓    ↓    ↓    ↓    ↓    ↓
wave:       ●    ●    ●    ●    ●    ●    ●
value:     100  500  900  700  200 -400 -800
```

Those numbers are the **samples**.

---

## So what does `500` mean?

Suppose we're using 16-bit PCM.

The possible values are approximately:

```text
-32768  ← minimum
    0   ← center
+32767  ← maximum
```

A sample of:

```text
+500
```

means:

> At this particular instant, the audio signal had a voltage/amplitude corresponding to +500.

A sample of:

```text
-15000
```

means the signal was far below the center point at that instant.

So the samples describe the **shape of the waveform**.

---

# How do numbers become sound again?

This is the really interesting part.

Suppose the computer has:

```text
0
5000
10000
5000
0
-5000
-10000
-5000
0
```

These numbers describe something like:

```text
       ●
      / \
     /   \
    ●     ●
   /       \
--●---------●--
             \
              ●
```

The computer sends these values to a **DAC** — Digital-to-Analog Converter.

The DAC converts the numbers into a continuously changing electrical voltage.

Conceptually:

```text
PCM samples
     ↓
   DAC
     ↓
changing voltage
     ↓
 amplifier
     ↓
 speaker
     ↓
moving speaker cone
     ↓
changing air pressure
     ↓
your ear
```

The speaker cone moves according to the changing electrical signal.

For example:

```text
sample value     speaker movement

 +10000              ↑
  +5000              ↑
      0              ●
  -5000              ↓
 -10000              ↓
      0              ●
```

The cone moves forward and backward, creating pressure changes in the air.

Your eardrum detects those pressure changes, and your brain interprets them as **sound**.

---

# Why do individual samples produce a smooth sound?

This is a key concept.

You might wonder:

> "If the computer only has separate points, wouldn't the speaker make little jumps?"

The DAC reconstructs a continuous signal from the samples, using filtering/interpolation.

For example:

```text
Samples:

       ●
      / \
     ●   ●
    /     \
---●-------●---
```

The reconstructed signal is approximately:

```text
       ╭──╮
      ╱    ╲
─────╯      ╰─────
```

So the speaker doesn't literally receive "sample 1, then stop, sample 2, then stop." It receives a continuously changing
analog signal.

---

## And frequency comes from how quickly the samples change

Consider these samples:

```text
0, 10000, 0, -10000, 0, 10000, 0, -10000...
```

The waveform is changing rapidly:

```text
    /\    /\
   /  \  /  \
--/----\/----\--
```

That's a **higher-frequency** sound.

Compare:

```text
0, 2000, 4000, 6000, 8000, 6000, 4000, 2000, 0...
```

The waveform changes more slowly:

```text
        /\
      /    \
_____/      \_____
```

That's a **lower-frequency** sound.

So:

- **Sample value** → where the waveform is vertically (amplitude)
- **Sample rate** → how frequently we measure it
- **Pattern of samples over time** → the actual waveform
- **Waveform's repetition rate** → frequency/pitch
- **Amplitude** → related to loudness

### One final analogy

Think of a sample as a **pixel**, but for time instead of space.

A pixel says:

> "At this position in the picture, the brightness/color is X."

A sample says:

> "At this moment in the audio, the signal amplitude is X."

Thousands of samples placed in sequence describe the entire audio waveform.
