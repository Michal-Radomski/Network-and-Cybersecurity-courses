The key idea is:

> **Samples do not individually contain a frequency. Frequency comes from the pattern of many samples over time.**

Suppose we have these PCM samples:

```text
0, 1000, 2000, 1000, 0, -1000, -2000, -1000, 0, ...
```

If we plot them:

```text
amplitude
   ↑
2000       ●
          / \
1000     ●   ●
        /     \
   0 ●---------●---------●
              \         /
-1000          ●       ●
                \     /
-2000             ●
```

This is approximately a **sine wave**.

### Where does frequency come from?

Frequency means **how many complete cycles happen per second**.

For example, suppose our samples are taken at **44,100 samples/second**.

If one complete waveform cycle occupies **441 samples**:

```text
441 samples = 1 cycle
```

then:

```text
44,100 samples/sec
────────────────── = 100 cycles/sec
     441 samples/cycle
```

So the sound is **100 Hz**.

### Another example

At 44.1 kHz:

**441 samples per cycle → 100 Hz**

**220.5 samples per cycle → 200 Hz**

**44.1 samples per cycle → 1000 Hz (1 kHz)**

So:

```text
More cycles per second → higher frequency
Fewer cycles per second → lower frequency
```

### But what if the sound isn't a pure sine wave?

Real sounds aren't usually one perfect frequency. For example, a musical instrument might contain:

```text
100 Hz     ← fundamental
200 Hz     ← harmonic
300 Hz     ← harmonic
500 Hz     ← another component
...
```

The PCM samples contain the **combined waveform** of all these components.

We can mathematically analyze that waveform using something like a **Fourier transform (FFT)**. The FFT looks at the pattern
of samples and tells us:

> "This waveform contains energy around 100 Hz, 200 Hz, 300 Hz, etc."

So the process is roughly:

```text
PCM samples
    ↓
waveform over time
    ↓
analyze pattern (FFT)
    ↓
frequencies
100 Hz, 200 Hz, 500 Hz, ...
```

And during playback, we don't actually have to calculate the frequency first. The DAC simply reconstructs the waveform
represented by the samples, and the speaker produces the corresponding pressure variations in the air. Your ear detects those
variations as frequencies.

**The most important distinction:** **Sample = amplitude at one moment.** **Frequency = how the amplitude pattern
repeats/changes over time.**
