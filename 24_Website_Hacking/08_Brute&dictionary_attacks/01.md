## What is a Brute Force Attack?

A **brute force attack** is a trial-and-error method where an automated script systematically guesses every possible
combination of characters until it hits the correct password or encryption key.

- **How it works:** The attacker's software tries combinations sequentially (from "aaaa" to "zzzz" and beyond) until access
  is granted.
- **The drawback for attackers:** For long, complex passwords, a pure brute force attack can take years or even centuries of
  computing power to crack.

---

## What is a Dictionary Attack?

A **dictionary attack** is a more targeted and efficient variation of a brute force attack. Instead of trying every random
character combination, the attacker uses a pre-compiled list—or "dictionary"—of common words, phrases, and previously leaked
passwords (such as "password123", "letmein", or "football").

- **Efficiency:** Because humans often choose predictable, easy-to-remember words, dictionary attacks can crack weak
  passwords in seconds.
- **Smart Variations:** Advanced dictionary attacks can automatically append numbers or substitute characters (like
  substituting `@` for `a`) to test common human habits.

---

## How to Defend Against These Attacks

Web developers and security systems use several layers of defense to stop automated guessing games:

- **Use Strong, Long Passwords:** Passwords with a mix of uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols require
  significantly more time and computing power to crack.
- **Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):** Adding a second verification step (like an authenticator app code) stops
  attackers dead in their tracks even if they successfully guess a password.
- **Implement Rate Limiting and Account Lockouts:** Servers can automatically slow down login responses or temporarily lock
  accounts after a few consecutive failed attempts, rendering automated scripts useless.
